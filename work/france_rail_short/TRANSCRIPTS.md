## 2. English dub — `..._v3_en_dubbed.mp3`

Why do all French trains seem to go through Paris? Take a look at this map of the French rail network. There's something pretty strange about it. To get from many French cities to another, the fastest route often seems to take you back through Paris. But why is the French network built like that?

We have to go back to the 19th century when France was massively developing its railways. Paris was already the country's political, economic, and demographic center, so the main lines were gradually organized to connect the capital to the different regions. As a result, the network took on an almost star-like shape around Paris.

And a century later, the TGV revolution further reinforced this logic. In 1981, the first high-speed line connected Paris to Lyon. Then came the routes to Lille, Bordeaux, Strasbourg, and Marseille. On a map, it's spectacular. The high-speed lines radiate out from the capital.

The problem arises when you want to travel between two major provincial cities. Some cross-country connections are less direct or slower, and going through Paris can sometimes remain competitive.

But the network is evolving. Lines already allow you to bypass Paris, and several projects are looking to improve connections between regions.

So the next time your train takes you up to Paris only to head back down, it's no coincidence. You are now following in the footsteps of nearly two centuries of the French railway's history.
